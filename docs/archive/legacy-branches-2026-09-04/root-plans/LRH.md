## 1. Notes (verbatim)

The common thread is: a Gaussian is a convenient mathematical model of "random variation with controlled scale." But the Gaussian serves two fundamentally different purposes in these papers:

| Paper | Gaussian's role | Question being answered |
|---|---|---|
| Relational transport | Random null baseline | Does the method recover real directional structure, or would arbitrary same-sized vectors perform similarly? |
| SIGReg/LeJEPA | Training target distribution | Can we prevent representation collapse by forcing features to remain high-entropy and isotropic? |
| Rectified L_p JEPA | Sparse training target | Can we retain anti-collapse properties while producing sparse, nonnegative features? |

Why Gaussians appear everywhere. A Gaussian has several unusually useful properties: Maximum entropy (among distributions with a fixed mean and covariance, the Gaussian makes the fewest additional assumptions; a principled model of "structureless variation given this scale"); No preferred direction (N(0,I) is isotropic; rotating the coordinate system does not change its distribution); Projection stability (any linear projection cᵀx of a Gaussian vector remains Gaussian); Analytical convenience (norms, angles, sums and projections have well-understood distributions); High-dimensional concentration (random Gaussian directions are almost orthogonal; for independent isotropic vectors in d dimensions, cosine similarity is approximately centered at zero with scale 1/√d).

The basic experimental logic is: preserve nuisance properties such as dimensionality and magnitude, destroy the hypothesized structure, and see whether performance disappears. But a Gaussian is not automatically a strong baseline. If real activations are anisotropic, heavy-tailed or clustered, an isotropic Gaussian matched only on norm may be too easy to beat. Stronger controls can match the empirical mean and covariance, randomly rotate real vectors, or permute the actual relational assignments.

What relational transport actually does. The paper defines a concept's contextual displacement as φ^(m)(w,τ) = r^(m)(w,τ) − r^(m)(w,τ₀), where r^(m)(w,τ₀) is the representation of word w in a neutral prompt, r^(m)(w,τ) is its representation in a context such as perception, affect or world knowledge, and φ is therefore the vector describing how context moved that word. Across approximately 1,000 words, these vectors form what the authors call a contextual transformation field. Different words are starting at different positions and moving in different directions. Relational transport asks whether the relationships among those movements are preserved across models. For a held-out word: in the source model, compare its displacement with every training word's displacement using cosine similarity; use those similarities as weights; in the target model, take the corresponding training-word displacements and combine them using the source-model weights; compare this synthesized target vector with the target model's true held-out displacement. Formally: φ̂^(m')(w) = Σ_j s_j^(m)(w) φ^(m')(w_j) / Σ_j |s_j^(m)(w)|. This is essentially signed kernel regression or barycentric interpolation in displacement space. The clever part is that it transports relations, not coordinates. Qwen and Llama can have different dimensions and arbitrarily rotated bases. The method never assumes that coordinate 183 in Qwen corresponds to coordinate 183 in Llama. It only assumes something like: if "apple" moves similarly to "peach" and "banana" in Qwen, then its Llama displacement should be reconstructible from how "peach" and "banana" move in Llama. One important limitation: this is not zero-shot transfer and not yet mechanistic localization. It observes the test word's displacement in the source model and uses known target-model training displacements. It demonstrates transferable relational organization, but does not identify which attention heads, MLPs or circuits generate it.

Why two random baselines? Random permutation keeps the real cosine weights and real target vectors but attaches weights to the wrong words; this tests whether the specific relational correspondence matters. Random Gaussian replaces the prediction with an essentially directionless vector at a comparable scale; this estimates the chance-level geometric floor. The permutation control is arguably the more informative one because it preserves more of the real data's marginal structure.

Residual cosine similarity. Ordinary cosine similarity would be cos(φ̂,φ) = φ̂ᵀφ/(‖φ̂‖‖φ‖). But suppose every concept displacement contains a strong generic template effect g: φ(w) = g + δ(w). Even a predictor that only learns g could obtain high cosine similarity, while completely failing to predict the word-specific component δ(w). The paper therefore subtracts the training-field mean φ̄_train and evaluates approximately cos(φ̂(w) − φ̄_train, φ(w) − φ̄_train). Averaging this over test words gives residual cosine similarity. Raw cosine asks, "Did both vectors point generally northeast?" Residual cosine asks, "After removing the general northeast drift, did the prediction correctly capture that this particular word veered farther east?" This is especially important because the paper finds that the field is neither one uniform direction nor fully random.

The paper's major geometric findings. 1. Contextual transformation is not a single direction: the first principal component explains only 7.3–14.6 % of displacement variance; consequently a single "apply perception context" vector cannot describe most words' contextual movement. At the same time, the global spherical variance is below that of uniformly random directions. So the accurate conclusion is: there is some shared global movement, but substantial, reproducible word-specific structure remains around it. 2. The heterogeneity is semantic: greater lexical density predicts smaller contextual displacement (ρ ≈ −0.22 to −0.32); greater concreteness predicts lower directional deviation (ρ ≈ −0.21 to −0.41). This is a geometric account of conceptual flexibility: abstract or semantically isolated concepts are more context-dependent. 3. Transformation geometry is shared across models: cross-model alignment of transformation fields tracks MMLU more strongly than parameter count (CKA vs MMLU r = 0.779; Grassmann vs MMLU r = 0.808). Relational transport then provides a predictive test: source-model relations predict held-out target-model displacement magnitudes and residual directions substantially better than the random controls. Across models, ordinary concept-space CKA is generally above 0.7 at matched relative depths. Real semantic contexts also outperform scrambled-context and Word2Vec/GloVe controls, with p < 0.0001 and rank-biserial effects above 0.82.

Why middle-to-late layers? Every block updates each token's residual-stream vector; there is no universal layer count (Qwen2.5-7B 28; Llama-3.1-8B 32; Ministral-3-8B 34; Gemma-2-9B 42; Qwen2.5-32B 64). Early layers: dominated by token identity, local form and shallow context. Middle layers: enough attention/MLP transformations have accumulated to integrate sentence context, meaning and compositional relationships. Final layers: increasingly specialized for next-token prediction and the unembedding/logit space. The brain-alignment claim (Caucheteux and King, 2022): linear mappings from model activations to held-out fMRI/MEG show an inverted-U with depth, layers around 50–75 % of depth predicting brain responses best; brain score correlates with language-model performance. "Brain alignment" should be read narrowly: the activations linearly predict some variance in measured brain responses.

How Rectified L_p JEPA uses Gaussian geometry. SIGReg uses Gaussians for training rather than evaluation. JEPA's invariance loss ‖z − z′‖² admits collapse; SIGReg prevents it by pushing random one-dimensional projections cᵀz toward a standard Gaussian (Cramér–Wold: if every one-dimensional projection matches, the full multivariate distributions match). Rectified L_p JEPA replaces the target with Y = ReLU(X), X ~ GeneralizedGaussian_p(μ,σ): a point mass at zero, a positive continuous tail, analytically controllable expected ℓ₀ sparsity, an L_p-dependent tail (p = 2 Gaussian-like; p = 1 Laplace-like; p < 1 sharper with heavier tails; μ/σ controls the mass at zero). Because rectified Gaussians are not closed under projection, they sample target vectors, project both learned and target samples, sort, and minimise the one-dimensional Wasserstein distance (RDMReg). This could help interpretability because sparse nonnegative codes are plausibly easier to decompose — but the paper demonstrates sparsity, independence and downstream performance, not that coordinates become clean human-interpretable features.

Is the new paper really the antithesis of the linear representation hypothesis? Not quite. It challenges a strong, overly static interpretation of LRH but does not disprove Park–Choe–Veitch. LRH: a factor such as male→female is a direction; embedding/unembedding geometry; counterfactual differences align; causal inner product for probes and interventions; static factor-of-variation geometry. Shared-geometry paper: a word forms a cloud across contexts; intermediate hidden activations; how context moves many concepts differently; basis-invariant CKA, subspace geometry, relational transport; dynamic vector-field geometry. Park et al. explicitly do not address intermediate-layer activations; LRH does not claim that every contextual transformation is one universal vector; a curved manifold can still contain locally linear feature directions. Synthesis: linear directions may describe some individual factors, while manifolds and structured vector fields describe how those factors interact with concepts and contexts.

The three genuinely distinct ideas: (1) concepts as point-cloud manifolds (a concept is a distribution of contextualized states with within- and between-concept geometry); (2) context as a vector field rather than one operator vector (the same framing moves different concepts in different directions and magnitudes, systematically related to concreteness and lexical density); (3) cross-model relational invariance and transport (what survives across architectures is the network of relations among concept positions and displacement vectors; relational transport turns that into a held-out prediction test). For mechanistic interpretability the third is probably the most valuable: compare models or checkpoints through their relational geometry and transition fields before searching for individual features. In the world-model setting the analogous object is φ(s,a) = z_{t+1} − z_t, a latent transition field indexed by state and action. One could test whether a poisoned or fine-tuned model preserves ordinary transition relations but selectively distorts the subfield around "locked," "collision" or "unsafe" states. Relational transport would diagnose that distortion; patching and ablation would then be needed to identify its causal mechanism.
