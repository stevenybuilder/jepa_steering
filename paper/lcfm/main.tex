\begin{abstract}
JEPA-WM chooses actions by predicting their consequences and scoring the predicted futures against a goal. We keep its checkpoints frozen and test how activation edits affect forecasts and planning. A learned rank-four predictor correction reduces six-step proprioceptive forecast MSE by 2.36\% on Reach and 2.19\% on Reach-Wall. In a separate comparison over 192 starting states, it changes the best of 300 candidate plans once; a cost-gap bound certifies unchanged choices in 184 states. Repeating the full adaptive search produces different plans under both learned and random edits, without an established advantage for the learned edit. A supporting experiment examines how an action-conditioning patch behaves as a two-frame context advances. Across sixteen development contexts, replacing both appearances of an action reproduces the changed-input forecast exactly; replacing only its first appearance matches initially but diverges later. The one-time patch selects a different candidate in 17 of 32 paired banks. This measures disagreement with a model reference, not better robot performance. Short physical replays and a 3,072-run protected evaluation leave improved task outcomes unresolved. The study measures forecast correction and its effects on planning within a fixed, short context window.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

JEPA-WM plans by forecasting candidate action sequences and choosing those whose predicted outcomes approach a goal \citep{terver2025jepawm}. We investigate whether correcting the predictor's activations improves those forecasts and changes the plans it selects. The encoders and predictor remain frozen. We compare learned low-rank corrections and visual/action-conditioning edits with matched random controls and component ablations.

Prediction error and planning cost ask different questions. Forecast error compares the predicted outcome of an action with what happened. The planner instead compares predicted futures with a goal and selects among competing actions. We measure both, then follow the selected actions through adaptive search and simulator execution. Separate development studies supply each diagnostic; a final protected evaluation tests task success on fresh scenarios.

An action-history experiment asks whether an internal patch reproduces the input change it is meant to represent. JEPA-WM uses each action in two consecutive context windows. Replacing its conditioning once can match the immediate forecast and then diverge when the original action returns as history. We compare that one-time replacement with replacement at both appearances, using a direct input-action change as the reference. The sixteen-context experiment tests this specific computation in a two-frame window. Whether the same issue occurs in models with longer contexts remains an open question.

Joseph et al.'s Physics Emergence Zone study probes physical variables across video-model layers and tests interventions on their representations \citep{joseph2026interpretingphysics}. Their approach motivates our layer sweeps and tests of representational geometry. We examine an action-conditioned predictor used by a planner, and include numerical-precision controls because the apparent geometry changes between FP32 and BF16.

\section{Related work}

DINO-WM and JEPA-WM use prediction in learned features for planning \citep{zhou2024dinowm,terver2025jepawm}. JEPA-WM studies which model and training choices support physical planning. We keep the released models fixed and ask what an inference-time activation correction changes.

Activation steering also appears in robot policies. COAST uses success- and failure-derived conceptors in a policy's action expert \citep{miao2026coast}. Our intervention enters a world-model predictor whose output is scored by a separate sampling planner. The supervision and computations differ, so comparing reported gains does not isolate the reason for their difference.

Joseph et al.\ probe physical variables and intervene on motion representations in video models \citep{joseph2026interpretingphysics}. Their layer analyses motivate checking where an internal signal is accessible and what changes when it is edited. Our six-layer action-conditioned predictor is a different object of study, and its attention maps do not identify a physics-emergence zone. Curved activation interpolation \citep{wurgaft2026manifoldsteering} motivates a second control: reconstruction quality should be tested against downstream prediction and numerical precision before receiving a physical interpretation.

\section{Model and experimental setup}

\paragraph{Prediction and planning.}
Frozen encoders map images and robot state to feature vectors. A six-block predictor takes these features and candidate actions to forecast six imagined steps. In the MetaWorld tasks, the planner minimizes
\begin{equation}
 C(a_{1:H})=\MSE(\hat z^v_H,z^v_g)+0.1\,\MSE(\hat z^p_H,z^p_g),
 \label{eq:main-cost}
\end{equation}
where $v$ and $p$ denote visual and robot-state features, and each MSE averages over its feature dimensions. The target is the goal, whereas forecast error compares a prediction with the outcome of those actions.

The Cross-Entropy Method (CEM) starts with 300 action sequences, keeps the ten with lowest predicted cost, and samples a new population around them. MetaWorld uses fifteen such iterations per planning call. The robot executes a three-step prefix, corresponding to fifteen elementary simulator actions, before replanning. Full MetaWorld episodes contain seven planning calls over 100 elementary steps; navigation uses thirty search iterations per call.

\paragraph{Interventions.}
The main learned edit adds a normalized correction in a four-dimensional subspace to the fourth predictor block's output (B3), at the third imagined step (H3). Its coefficients come from an offline fitted map of the current unedited features. A random-subspace comparison matches rank, edit site, and prescribed dose, with separate response calibration. Both are activation edits; the robot still obtains its actions from the planner.

We also test a learned pair of visual-input and action-conditioning directions. Equal-budget and randomized versions form one comparison. Unscaled joint, visual-only, and action-only edits form a component ablation. Together with unsteered and the two four-direction conditions, these give eight arms. Appendix~\ref{sec:design} gives the equations, fitting details, and dose definitions. No intervention updates the base model weights.

\paragraph{Data and uncertainty.}
Fitting trajectories determine the operators. Separate recorded development trajectories measure prediction error and inform the recipe. Development diagnostics compare fixed plans, iterative searches, and short physical executions. The protected evaluation uses 96 new scenarios per task on Reach, Reach-Wall, PointMaze, and Wall after freezing the eight-arm recipe. All arms share the same physical GPU within each scenario, with strict FP32 and TF32 disabled. Engineering checks use excluded inputs.

Statistical units are source trajectories for recorded forecasts and starting scenarios for planning and behavior. Candidate plans are not independent replicates. Registered uncertainty uses paired bootstrap resampling and correction across the specified comparisons: six for the search extension, twelve for physical replay, and 48 for protected behavior. Each uses 20,000 draws. Details and source identities are retained in the appendices and accompanying reports. Push-T and DROID appear only in earlier development results; DROID measures recorded-action agreement.

\section{Results}

\subsection{The learned edit improves some forecasts}

On recorded development trajectories, the four-direction edit reduces H6 robot-state embedding MSE by 2.360\% on Reach and 2.192\% on Reach-Wall. These are prediction errors for recorded futures. They do not measure how the robot moves under the edited planner.

An earlier rank-one sweep finds larger H6 forecast corrections at early predictor blocks on both tasks, in FP32 and BF16. Push-T does not show the same pattern. The sweep uses independently fitted rank-one operators; it does not test moving the later four-direction edit to another layer. Appendix~\ref{sec:layers} retains the full layer analysis.

\subsection{A score-margin bound characterizes fixed-bank selection}

We next score identical banks of 300 action sequences in 192 development states, 96 per MetaWorld task. The learned edit changes the winning plan in zero Reach states and one Reach-Wall state.

To assess why a winner can remain stable, let $m$ be the original cost gap between the best and second-best plans. Let $\delta_i$ be the cost change for plan $i$, and let $r=\max_i\delta_i-\min_i\delta_i$ be the range of these changes. If $r<m$, no competitor can overtake the winner: its original disadvantage exceeds any relative improvement the edit can give it. Appendix~\ref{sec:decision} gives the proof and all four edit comparisons.

This condition holds for the learned edit in 184 of 192 states: 94 Reach and 90 Reach-Wall. Failure of the bound does not imply a changed winner. Conversely, preserving the best plan does not preserve all ten elites used by CEM. The bound applies to the saved finite set of candidates, and does not certify the result of later search iterations.

A separate 64-state replay asks which part of the edit produces the score changes. Keeping only the correction shared across candidates reconstructs the full edit's relative cost changes with scores of 0.983 on Reach and 0.998 on Reach-Wall. Random edits show a similar pattern. A shared activation correction can still change relative costs because squared goal distance depends on each candidate's starting prediction (Equation~\ref{eq:translation}). Components retain their natural magnitudes, so this test does not compare their efficacy at equal energy.

\subsection{Iterative search can return different plans}

CEM changes its next candidate population using the current elite plans. Small score changes can therefore alter later searches even when the first winner remains unchanged. A 56-state extension, with 28 states per task, runs all fifteen iterations for unsteered, learned, and random-subspace conditions.

Both edits change the returned action prefixes. Mean RMS differences from unsteered are 0.697/0.578 for learned/random on Reach and 0.500/0.217 on Reach-Wall, in normalized action coordinates. All six registered learned-minus-random intervals include zero. These measurements establish changed plans, but do not establish an advantage for the learned edit or measure physical displacement. Complete search curves and contrasts appear in Appendix~\ref{sec:replay}.

\subsection{Physical replay separates forecast accuracy from plan quality}

We execute each condition's selected prefix from the same simulator reset in all 56 extension states. Each trajectory contains fifteen elementary actions. A repeated unsteered execution checks exact reset and trajectory agreement, giving 224 trajectories. All three models also predict all three selected plans, giving 504 model/plan forecasts.

Holding the unsteered-selected actions fixed, the learned edit reduces weighted forecast MSE by 0.002720 on Reach (simultaneous 95\% interval $[0.002342,0.003061]$) and 0.002268 on Reach-Wall ($[0.001905,0.002607]$). These are absolute error reductions. For executed plans, however, all eight contrasts in physical end-effector distance and actual encoded-goal cost include zero under the registered twelve-comparison correction (Figure~\ref{fig:physical-prefix}).

Thus we can detect better same-action forecasts in this experiment without establishing that the selected actions bring the robot closer to its goal. This tests short executions on development states. It is separate from full-task success. CEM optimizes H6 while this follow-up measures the H3 endpoint, so discrepancies here do not by themselves show exploitation of the optimized objective.

\subsection{A one-time action patch diverges when the context advances}
\label{sec:main-history}

We replace the H3 action with a donor action and use the resulting raw-input rollout as the counterfactual reference. The predictor's action encoder maps 20 action coordinates into a 400-dimensional condition. At H3 the changed action occupies the newest context position; at H4 it occupies the older position. We compare condition replacement at H3 only with replacement at both H3 and H4. The experiment retains sixteen development contexts (eight each on Reach and Reach-Wall), original and freshly seeded candidate banks, and all six predictor blocks, for 1,120 H6 forecasts. Candidate banks share contexts and are not independent samples.

Let $\hat z_{\rm cf}$ denote the coherent raw-action forecast and $\hat z_{\rm native}$ the unmodified forecast. At each measured endpoint we report
\begin{equation}
 R=1-\frac{D(\hat z_{\rm patch},\hat z_{\rm cf})}{D(\hat z_{\rm native},\hat z_{\rm cf})},
 \label{eq:history-main}
\end{equation}
where $D$ averages candidate-wise visual MSE plus 0.1 times proprioceptive MSE before taking the ratio. $R=1$ is exact counterfactual reconstruction and $R=0$ is the unmodified forecast's error. This measures agreement with another model rollout, not with the physical future.

Replacing both appearances at all six blocks reproduces the raw-action substitution byte-exactly through H6. Replacing all blocks only at H3 matches at H3, then diverges when the original action returns as history at H4. Its H6 reconstruction is $R=0.5029$--$0.5079$ across the four task/bank cells (Figure~\ref{fig:main-history}). Exact all-block parity is a computational consistency control: replacing every occurrence reconstructs the changed input's computation. The H3-only comparison measures the consequence of omitting the action's second appearance.

Single-block interventions vary by layer. The improvement from replacing both appearances has intervals above zero at B0--B3 in both tasks and both banks: sixteen of twenty-four cells under the registered simultaneous correction. B4/B5 remain unresolved. B1 has the largest observed persistence difference, $\Delta R=0.4991$--$0.5057$; its persistent H6 reconstruction is $0.7320$--$0.7421$, versus $0.2286$--$0.2387$ for H3-only. B1 is an illustrative post hoc choice, not a newly selected steering site. Appendix~\ref{sec:history} retains all six layers, input-range controls, and all 72 primary contrasts.

\paragraph{The mismatch reaches candidate selection.}
A post hoc reanalysis of the saved H6 costs compares each patch with the coherent raw-action reference. For all-block H3-only patches, mean Spearman agreement is 0.878--0.894 and mean top-ten overlap is 5.75--6.88 across task/bank cells. Their winning candidate differs from the reference in 17 of 32 banks (two banks per context); persistent all-block patches agree exactly. The independent sample remains sixteen contexts. A different selected candidate is neither a task-success event nor evidence of a better plan. B1 persistence improves mean rank agreement and elite overlap in every task/bank cell, while winner agreement improves in three of four. The candidate selected by the one-time all-block patch has 2.53\% to 3.92\% higher cost under the reference on average across the four task/bank groups, including unchanged selections. This percentage divides each context's excess reference cost by its minimum reference cost before averaging. These descriptive comparisons retain all sites and contexts. They measure fixed-bank selection, not a new CEM or physical rollout (Appendix~\ref{app:history-ranking}).

The result supports checking later forecasts when an activation patch is intended to reproduce an input change. Exact agreement at the edited step does not establish agreement after the context advances. The all-block repeated replacement is a consistency control. The experiment does not test a method for improving physical control, and its sixteen contexts provide limited evidence about how frequently candidate choices change in other states.

\subsection{Local geometry depends on how the predictor is computed}

With weights, inputs, and interpolation coefficients fixed across 64 contexts, cubic activation reconstruction outperforms linear reconstruction at every block in FP32; BF16 reverses the comparison. Rounding only the FP32 output does not reproduce actual BF16 computation. A separate five-task interpolation study finds small, mixed downstream forecast benefits after matching requested edit doses. An apparent geometric advantage therefore needs numerical controls and a separate test of its effect on prediction (Appendix~\ref{sec:geometry}).

\subsection{Protected behavioral evaluation}
\label{sec:behavior}

The protected panel evaluates eight arms on 384 fresh scenarios across four tasks, for 3,072 runs. Table~\ref{tab:main-rates} gives every arm. Steering does change which scenarios succeed. On Reach, unsteered and learned steering each succeed on 52 of 96 scenarios. The edit rescues 21 failures and loses 21 successes. Earlier development rates use different scenarios and planner randomness, and are not substitutes for the concurrent protected baseline.

\section{Discussion and limitations}

The learned correction improves some forecasts, but a planner responds to relative goal costs. In the fixed candidate banks, the correction rarely changes the lowest-cost plan. The cost-gap bound accounts for most of these stable choices. CEM can still change its later proposals and returned actions under both learned and random edits. The physical replay detects lower prediction error for the same actions while leaving differences in goal attainment unresolved.

The history experiment has a narrower interpretation. Its reference is the forecast produced by a changed input action. Replacing the corresponding conditioning everywhere reproduces that computation, as expected. Omitting the historical appearance changes later forecasts and sometimes the selected candidate. The 17/32 count describes two banks within each of sixteen contexts; it is not a success rate or 32 independent observations. We have not shown that the disagreement causes worse physical outcomes. The fixed two-frame window also limits what this experiment says about longer-context models.

A follow-up should first estimate the frequency and size of these selection changes on new independent trajectories. It should pair conditions within each trajectory and report uncertainty across trajectories, keeping additional candidate banks as repeated measurements. Testing a planning benefit requires a specified steering rule, concurrent unsteered and matched random controls, and simulator outcomes. Sample size should follow the smallest useful effect and variability of the paired outcomes. A larger replication of exact computational agreement alone would not establish a better planning method. These are proposed experiments; the present paper reports the completed sixteen-context study.

Our physical follow-up executes three selected plans per state and measures H3 while CEM optimizes H6. It therefore cannot recover the physical ranking of all 300 candidates. The protected evaluation uses 96 scenarios per task, one released checkpoint per task, and task-specific fitted edits, without independent training-seed replication. All 48 simultaneous contrast intervals include zero, leaving small behavioral effects unresolved. Because the development diagnostics and protected episodes use different inputs and randomness, the diagnostics do not identify the cause of individual protected outcomes.

\section{Conclusion}

Activation edits in frozen JEPA-WM can reduce forecast error and change the plans returned by adaptive search. In the measured fixed candidate banks, the learned correction usually leaves the first choice unchanged, with a simple cost-gap bound certifying most cases. The action-history check shows why an internal replacement should be evaluated after the context advances: a one-time patch can reproduce an immediate forecast and miss the later effect of the same input change. Establishing a control benefit requires comparisons against simulator outcomes; the completed behavioral evaluation does not establish that benefit.

\section{Intervention details and data populations}
\label{sec:design}

\paragraph{Frozen predictor and planner.}
We use released JEPA-WM checkpoints and task-specific fitted interventions. Reach and Reach-Wall share a checkpoint, not their fitted operators. DINO visual features and proprioceptive features feed a six-block action-conditioned predictor. All base encoders and predictor weights remain frozen during intervention; this does not imply that every encoder was frozen during original training.

The analyzed MetaWorld planner uses the actual weighted goal cost
\begin{equation}
 C(a_{1:H})=\MSE(\hat z^v_H,z^v_g)+0.1\,\MSE(\hat z^p_H,z^p_g),
 \label{eq:cost}
\end{equation}
with each MSE averaged over its modality's dimensions. The cost is \emph{not} visual-only. Recorded-future proprioceptive error and goal-directed planning cost nevertheless use different targets, weights, and action inputs. CEM uses 300 candidates. MetaWorld uses 15 optimization iterations and seven planning calls over the recorded 100-elementary-step episodes; navigation uses 30 iterations. Imagined steps, simulator steps, CEM iterations, and replanning calls are distinct units.

\paragraph{Refined rank-four correction.}
The later fixed-response operator edits the B3 output at H3. With native field \(h\), orthonormal four-direction basis \(B\), and \(\phi(h)\) consisting of an intercept and three standardized native-feature scores, an offline map \(W\) gives
\begin{equation}
 d(h)=BW\phi(h),\qquad h'=h+\eta\,d(h)/\|d(h)\|_2,
 \label{eq:refined}
\end{equation}
using the frozen zero-edit rule below the correction threshold. The edit covers all 256 visual patches within the existing batch, without online response probes or native-shadow forecasts. Its random-subspace comparator has separate response calibration and matched support, rank, basis-projector spectrum, and prescribed dose---not identical coefficient-map singular values. This successor is not an equivalent implementation of the earlier online response solver.

\paragraph{Visual/action interventions.}
A fit-only maximum-covariance direction pair edits the H3 visual predictor input and B3 action-conditioning vector. Unscaled joint, visual-only, and action-only arms form a fixed-dose factorial. Equal-budget coupling scales both standardized doses by \(1/\sqrt{2}\); its random-direction comparator uses the same sites and dose policy. This comparison is distinct from the unscaled factorial (Table~\ref{fig:arms}).

Fit trajectories supply directions, readouts, and calibration. Separate recorded-action development lineages measure forecast effects and inform choices; fitting-disjoint does not mean selection-protected. \section{Candidate scores, component replay, and search}
\label{sec:decision}

\paragraph{An exact sufficient certificate.}
For a fixed candidate bank, let \(C_i\) be native cost, \(i^\star\) its unique winner, and \(\delta_i=C'_i-C_i\) the edit-induced change. Define
\begin{equation}
 m=\min_{j\ne i^\star}(C_j-C_{i^\star}),\qquad
 r=\max_i\delta_i-\min_i\delta_i.
 \label{eq:margin}
\end{equation}
If \(r<m\), the winner cannot change, because every competitor satisfies
\begin{equation}
 C'_j-C'_{i^\star}
 =(C_j-C_{i^\star})+(\delta_j-\delta_{i^\star})
 \ge m-r>0.
 \label{eq:certificate}
\end{equation}
This sufficient inequality concerns the saved finite bank, not generalization. Failure to certify does not imply a flip; actual selection and tie handling are checked separately.
The diagnostic selects the bank's best candidate. CEM instead updates its proposal from ten elites, so preserving the best index alone does not certify an unchanged CEM update or final plan.

\paragraph{Measured cost changes are small relative to ranking.}
We analyze all 192 completed development scenarios and four non-native diagnostic arms (768 paired comparisons). For refined editing, \(r<m\) in 94/96 Reach and 90/96 Reach-Wall banks; winners actually change in 0/96 and 1/96. The mean scenario-level ratio
\[
 100\,\RMS_i(\delta_i-\bar\delta)/\sqrt{\Var_i(C_i)}
\]
is 0.2466\% and 0.2786\%. Coupling certifies 82/96 and 81/96 banks and changes 1/96 and 2/96 winners. Table~\ref{tab:margin} retains both random controls.

\paragraph{Shared coefficients need not produce shared costs.}
A separate decomposition of 768 protected arm records finds that 98.855\% and 99.615\% of refined coefficient energy on Reach and Reach-Wall is shared across candidates within a call. This is coefficient geometry, not the fraction of decision-irrelevant information. Even a constant final-latent translation \(d\) changes squared goal cost by
\begin{equation}
 \|z_i+d-g\|^2-\|z_i-g\|^2
 =2d^\top(z_i-g)+\|d\|^2,
 \label{eq:translation}
\end{equation}
whose first term varies across candidates. The actual edit also passes through a nonlinear predictor. Margin measurements therefore add information that coefficient energy alone cannot supply.

Archived decision banks and protected episodes differ in inputs and planner RNG. Protected records did not retain numeric candidate costs or elite identities. Thus the certificate cannot explain which protected scenarios were rescued or broken. A low first-bank flip count also does not establish replanning drift as their cause.

\subsection{Component replay and actual adaptive search}
\label{sec:replay}

\paragraph{A shared field carries relative-score changes.}
We test the shared-correction question on all 64 fixed development contexts. For each learned or calibrated-random correction, cache its native H3/B3 field and replay the full field, zero field, candidate-common mean, and candidate-centered remainder on the same 300 actions. Cached-full and zero controls reproduce their reference forecasts and scores exactly. Neither component is renormalized, and no operator is refitted.

Let $u$ be the full intervention's cost change, centered across candidates, and $u_k$ the corresponding change for component $k$. We report $R_k=1-\|u-u_k\|_2^2/\|u\|_2^2$, retaining undefined zero-denominator cases and allowing negative values. This is a reconstruction score, not a probability. For learned edits, common-only $R$ is 0.9827 on Reach (marginal 95\% interval $[0.9793,0.9858]$) and 0.9975 on Reach-Wall ($[0.9971,0.9980]$); centered-only values are 0.0218 and 0.0135. Common-field energy fractions are 99.53\% and 99.78\%. Random-subspace common-only scores are similarly high, 0.9795 and 0.9854. These results establish the common component's effect at its natural energy, not superiority over equal-energy centered steering.

\section{Numerical precision and activation reconstruction}
\label{sec:geometry}

\paragraph{A controlled arithmetic comparison.}
The 64-context experiment fixes model weights, encoded inputs, the literal first action coordinate, four symmetric perturbations ($\pm0.1,\pm0.05$), and reconstruction coefficients. At every block, it compares strict FP32, actual BF16-autocast computation, and FP32 fields rounded to BF16 then restored to FP32. Each condition reconstructs its own unperturbed center; no fitted bank or dose-normalized correction changes between conditions.

FP32 favors cubic reconstruction at all six blocks: mean log cubic/linear MSE ratios span $-5.40$ to $-7.89$. Output round-tripping moves these ratios near equality ($-0.098$ to $+0.010$), whereas actual BF16 produces $+0.160$ to $+0.306$. The actual-BF16-minus-rounded contrast is $+0.224$ to $+0.336$ across all twelve task/block cells, with six-layer-corrected lower bounds above the prespecified 0.1 log-unit margin. Thus output rounding degrades the apparent reconstruction advantage but does not quantitatively reproduce the actual BF16 result. The experiment isolates numerical-condition sensitivity, not an individual kernel or a physical manifold.

\paragraph{Precision-sensitive local interpolation.}
The action-response experiment withholds the center of a four-donor activation path. Coordinates \(-1,-\tfrac12,\tfrac12,1\) index frozen local action offsets. Equal-anchor linear reconstruction averages the donors. The archived cubic estimator at zero is
\begin{equation}
 \hat h_{\mathrm{center}}=-\tfrac16h_{-1}
 +\tfrac23h_{-1/2}+\tfrac23h_{1/2}-\tfrac16h_1.
 \label{eq:center}
\end{equation}
At this symmetric evaluation point the same functional is obtained by quadratic least squares. It does not uniquely identify third-order dynamics or a dense activation manifold.

Across all five tasks, raw omitted-activation MSE is 35.15--46.77\% \emph{higher} than linear reconstruction in BF16, but 93.96--99.99\% \emph{lower} in FP32. This does not correspond to a similar improvement in future prediction. Under matched requested doses, the H6 proprioceptive-MSE advantage over the linear arm is small and mixed: BF16 task means range from \(-0.0213\%\) to \(+0.0264\%\) of native future MSE; FP32 ranges from \(-0.0141\%\) to \(+0.0051\%\). The two endpoints have different targets and denominators (Appendix~\ref{app:geometry}), so these scales are not an attenuation coefficient.

\paragraph{Requested dose is not exact realized-energy equivalence.}
The precision conditions also differ in fitted banks and prescribed dose, preventing attribution solely to rounding. In FP32 Reach/Reach-Wall, the cubic arm's per-window realized-energy tolerance passes only 49.0\%/50.7\% of windows, versus 98.1\%/98.4\% for linear reconstruction, despite small mean realized/requested L2 discrepancies. The comparison is therefore labelled by its actual requested-dose contract, not perfect per-window energy matching.

\paragraph{Output nonadditivity differs from loss interaction.}
For the unscaled visual/action factorial, let \(e=z_0-y\), \(v=z_V-z_0\), \(a=z_A-z_0\), and \(q=z_{VA}-z_V-z_A+z_0\). Squared-error interaction obeys
\begin{equation}
 L(VA)-L(V)-L(A)+L(0)
 =2v^\top a+2(e+v+a)^\top q+\|q\|^2,
 \label{eq:factorial}
\end{equation}
with the endpoint's dimensional averaging. Thus loss interaction can be nonzero even when output interaction \(q\) vanishes.

Saved H6 output-interaction squared norms, divided by native proprioceptive MSE, are 0.419\%, 0.512\%, and 5.020\% in BF16 for Reach, Reach-Wall, and Push-T. Corresponding FP32 values are \(4.82\times10^{-6}\%\), \(1.07\times10^{-5}\%\), and \(0.00256\%\). These establish precision-conditioned differences in retained scalar diagnostics, not that rounding caused them. Missing horizons remain missing; original tensors are not reconstructed from scalars. Together, these results distinguish local interpolation quality, the delivered activation intervention, and its downstream forecast utility.
The loss terms can nearly cancel: BF16 Push-T has a \(+2.460\%\) additive cross-term and \(-2.509\%\) nonadditive remainder, yielding only \(-0.049\%\) net H6 loss interaction. Figure~\ref{fig:factorial} retains all seven source conditions, not just this illustrative case.
